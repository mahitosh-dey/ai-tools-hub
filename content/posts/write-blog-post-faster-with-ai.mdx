I want to be upfront about something before we start. "10x faster" is a real number for me, but it comes with a caveat. The speed gain is not from letting AI write your posts wholesale and hitting publish. That approach produces content that sounds like AI wrote it, because it did, and readers notice, Google notices, and your credibility takes a quiet hit.

The speed gain comes from using AI for the parts of writing that eat time without adding value: the blank-page paralysis, the research organization, the structural thinking, the first draft of sections you already know what you want to say. Your job shifts from generating every sentence to directing, editing, and improving. That is genuinely faster and, in my experience, produces better posts than doing it all manually.

Here is my exact process, tested across two years of AI-assisted publishing.

## Step 1: Start with a clear topic and target keyword

Before you touch any AI tool, spend five minutes getting clear on two things: what the post is specifically about, and what search phrase you want it to rank for.

This matters because vague instructions produce vague output. "Write me a blog post about AI tools" gives you something generic. "Write a 1,500-word beginner's guide to using ChatGPT for email writing, targeting the keyword 'use ChatGPT for emails'" gives the AI something to work with.

If you do not know your target keyword yet, use a free tool like Google's "People also ask" sections, AnswerThePublic, or type your topic into Google and see what autocomplete suggests. You are looking for the phrase real people actually type when they are searching for what you are writing about. Paid tools like Ahrefs, Semrush, or Keyword Insights give you more data, but a Google search plus the "related searches" at the bottom of the results is enough to start.

Write that keyword down before you open ChatGPT. It anchors everything that follows. Also write down the searcher's intent, meaning what the person typing that keyword actually wants. Are they trying to learn something? Buy something? Compare options? Get a quick answer? A post that matches intent ranks. A post that misses intent does not, no matter how well written.

## Step 2: Use AI to build your outline

This is where I save the most time. Outlines used to take me 20 to 30 minutes of thinking and restructuring. Now I do it in about five.

Open [ChatGPT](https://chat.openai.com) or [Claude](https://claude.ai) (both work well for this) and use a prompt like this:

"I am writing a blog post for beginners titled '[your title]'. The target keyword is '[your keyword]'. The reader wants to know [what they are trying to accomplish]. Give me a detailed outline with H2 and H3 sections that covers the topic thoroughly and flows logically. Aim for a post around [word count] words. Include a section on common mistakes and a short FAQ section at the end."

The AI will give you a structure. It will not be perfect. AI outlines tend to be thorough but sometimes miss the angle or emphasis that makes a post genuinely useful. Read through it critically. Move sections around, cut anything redundant, add anything it missed based on your own knowledge of the topic.

You are not accepting the outline as-is. You are using it as a starting point to think against, which is much faster than building from nothing.

Extra step that helps: paste the top three ranking posts for your keyword into the same chat and ask the AI to identify what they cover and what they miss. That gives you an outline that includes the standard coverage plus your own differentiators. The posts that rank in position 4 to 10 usually have gaps you can fill with the right angles.

## Step 3: Research and fact-check before you write

This step comes before writing, not after. A common mistake is generating AI content and then trying to verify it. Do it the other way round.

For any factual claims your post will need, statistics, pricing, feature comparisons, historical context, research those yourself first using reliable sources. Use [Perplexity AI](https://www.perplexity.ai) if you want AI-assisted research, because it cites its sources and you can click through to verify. For anything that matters, check the original source directly. The Perplexity citation is a starting point, not a finish line.

Write down the verified facts and figures you want to include. When you later use AI to draft sections, you will paste these in and tell it to use them rather than letting the AI generate its own numbers. This is how you avoid hallucinated statistics ending up in your published post.

AI tools including ChatGPT will confidently state incorrect statistics if you do not provide the real ones. Never let the AI be your source of record for facts. I keep a simple text file called "facts.md" open while writing, with each verified claim and a link to its source. When I paste text into a Claude prompt for drafting, I paste the relevant facts from that file so the model works from confirmed data.

For a deeper research workflow, see the [Perplexity AI review](/blog/perplexity-ai-review) which covers Spaces, Deep Research, and how to build a project-based research setup for long-form articles.

## Step 4: Draft section by section, not all at once

Asking AI to write your entire post in one go produces the worst output. The result is a long piece of generic, evenly-weighted text that reads like a Wikipedia article someone turned up the enthusiasm on.

Instead, draft one section at a time. For each H2 section, write a focused prompt that tells the AI:

- What the section is about
- What specific point you want it to make
- Any facts or examples to include
- The tone you want (conversational, technical, funny, direct)
- Roughly how long it should be
- Any words or phrases you want it to avoid

For example: "Write the section on 'how to use ChatGPT for outlines'. Keep it practical and first-person, like someone explaining from experience. The key point is that AI outlines are a starting point to think against, not a finished product. Use these facts: [pasted facts]. Avoid the words 'delve,' 'dive in,' and 'in today's fast-paced world.' About 200 words."

That prompt produces something usable. A prompt that says "write the outline section of my blog post" produces something generic.

Go through each section of your outline this way. Some sections will come out well and need minimal editing. Others will miss the mark and you will rewrite them yourself faster than you would revise the AI output. Both are fine. You are moving faster than starting from a blank page either way.

A tip that saves time: keep a "voice profile" prompt in your notes app that describes your writing style with 2 or 3 sample paragraphs. Paste it at the top of every drafting chat. Claude in particular picks up voice cues quickly and outputs drafts that need much less editing.

## Step 5: Write the introduction and conclusion yourself

I write these myself every time. Not because AI cannot write introductions, it can, but because the introduction is where you establish your voice and make a real person want to keep reading. AI introductions tend to start with "In today's digital landscape..." or some variation of that energy. Readers have developed a nose for it, and Google's helpful content signals have too.

Your introduction should do one thing: make someone who just searched for your topic feel like they have found the right place. That usually means acknowledging something true about their situation, establishing your credibility without bragging about it, and getting to the point quickly. Skip the throat clearing. Skip the historical background. Get into the first useful sentence within a paragraph.

The conclusion is where you tell the reader what to do next. Again, this needs your voice and your specific opinion, not a generic AI summary of the post they just read.

Two minutes on a real human introduction beats two paragraphs of polished AI throat-clearing.

## Step 6: Edit with Grammarly and your own eyes

Once you have a full draft, paste it into [Grammarly](https://www.grammarly.com). The free tier is enough for this. See the [Grammarly review](/blog/grammarly-review-2026) to decide if Premium is worth it for you. Grammarly will catch grammar errors, awkward sentence structures, and punctuation issues you have stopped seeing after staring at the document.

Then read the whole thing out loud. This sounds tedious but takes about five minutes for a 1,500-word post, and it catches two things Grammarly misses: sentences that are technically correct but rhythmically wrong, and places where the post stops sounding like you.

Anywhere it sounds robotic, stilted, or like nobody would actually say that, rewrite it in your own words. This editing pass is where your voice reasserts itself over the AI's draft, and it is the step that separates content that reads well from content that just exists.

Third editing pass I now add: run a "humanizer check" specifically looking for AI tells. Kill em dashes. Cut phrases like "in today's fast-paced world" and "it is important to note." Look for the "not just X, but Y" pattern and rewrite it. Look for uniform paragraph lengths and break them up. This takes 5 to 10 minutes but noticeably lifts the finished quality.

## Step 7: Add your own experience and examples

This is the step most people skip, and it is the most important one for long-term content quality.

AI can write about topics. It cannot write about your experience. Every post is stronger if it contains at least one specific, real thing: something you tried that did or did not work, a result you actually got, a mistake you made, a client situation you navigated. These details cannot be fabricated convincingly, and readers respond to them in a way they do not respond to general advice.

Even one paragraph of genuine first-hand experience per post changes how it reads. It also changes how it performs. Google has been explicit about rewarding "experience, expertise, authoritativeness, and trustworthiness" (E-E-A-T). AI-only content does not have the first one.

Add your examples after the AI draft. They do not need to be long. They need to be true.

Concrete formats that work: a short anecdote in italics, a callout box with "what I actually did," a data table with your own numbers, a screenshot of a real result you got, or a comment from a client you can share with permission. Any of these signals real experience to a reader within seconds.

## What a realistic workflow looks like

Here is what this process actually looks like in terms of time for a standard 1,500-word post:

| Step | Time |
|---|---|
| Define topic and keyword | 5 minutes |
| Generate and refine outline | 10 minutes |
| Research and collect facts | 15 to 20 minutes |
| Draft sections with AI | 20 to 25 minutes |
| Write intro and conclusion | 10 minutes |
| Edit and read aloud | 15 minutes |
| Add personal experience | 10 minutes |
| Total | around 90 minutes |

Before using this process, a 1,500-word post took me most of a working day, from blank-page anxiety to something I was willing to publish. The bottleneck was always the starting and the structuring, not the actual writing. AI removes both of those bottlenecks while keeping the quality decisions in your hands.

For a 3,000-word cornerstone post, the workflow scales linearly. Plan on 3 to 4 hours total. That is still much faster than a full day of manual writing, and the quality is comparable or better because you are spending your time on the parts that matter.

## Common mistakes that slow you down

Three mistakes I made early on and see other writers make constantly.

Prompting too generally. "Write a blog post about X" is not a prompt. "Write section 2 of a beginner's guide about X for busy freelancers, using this specific angle, in this specific voice, avoiding these specific words" is a prompt. The quality of the output tracks the quality of the prompt almost linearly.

Not saving reusable prompts. If you write a lot, you should have a "prompt library" in Notion or a text file with the drafting prompts that consistently produce good results. Copying and refining an existing prompt is 3x faster than writing a new one every time.

Editing too little. The 20-minute drafting phase is where AI helps. The 25-minute editing phase is where the post becomes yours. Skimping on editing is the single biggest reason AI-assisted content sounds like AI. Do not skip it. If you do not have time to edit properly, delay the post.

## What AI cannot replace

Since we are being honest: AI cannot give your post an original perspective, a genuine opinion, or first-hand credibility. It can produce well-structured, grammatically correct, readable text about almost any topic. What it produces is not inherently insightful.

The blogs that build real audiences, the ones people bookmark and come back to, have a point of view. They say things that are slightly inconvenient or counterintuitive. They admit uncertainty. They share what actually happened, not just what the theory says should happen.

AI gets you to a draft faster. It does not get you to a perspective. That part is still on you, and it is the part that matters most.

Use the time you save to think harder about what you actually believe about your topic. Then write that. That is what people cannot get anywhere else.
