On Wednesday, GPT-5.5 stops working in ChatGPT.

Not everywhere. OpenAI's own documentation is specific about this in a way that is easy to misread:

> On October 14, 2026, GPT-5.5 will retire from ChatGPT, ChatGPT Work, and Codex on all plans.

and then, two lines later:

> This retirement does not apply to the OpenAI API.

So the same model name keeps working in your code and stops working in the product. If you have been testing against the API and assuming the app behaves the same way, Wednesday is the day those two stop agreeing.

Nine models have already been retired this year across these two companies, on six separate dates. GPT-5.5 makes ten. I have yet to meet anyone who noticed more than one of them.

## Every model retirement in 2026

Both of these came from the vendors' own deprecation pages, read on 12 October 2026.

| Date | Model | Vendor |
|---|---|---|
| 19 February | claude-3-5-haiku, claude-3-7-sonnet | Anthropic |
| 20 April | claude-3-haiku | Anthropic |
| 15 June | claude-opus-4, claude-sonnet-4 | Anthropic |
| 5 August | claude-opus-4-1 | Anthropic |
| 31 August | gpt-5.4, gpt-5.4-mini (Codex) | OpenAI |
| 14 September | GPT-5.3-Codex-Spark | OpenAI |
| **14 October** | **GPT-5.5** (not the API) | OpenAI |
| 30 November | claude-sonnet-4-5 | Anthropic |

Eight dates, nine models already gone and two retirements still ahead of you. Claude Sonnet 4.5 goes on 30 November, which happens to be the same day Mozilla Solo deletes every website it hosts. November is going to be a bad month for things you assumed would still be there.

## Where this actually hurts

Nobody loses a weekend because a dropdown changed. The damage is in the places that run without you watching.

OpenAI's migration note lists exactly where to look, and the list is worth reading slowly:

> workspace defaults, saved model settings, managed configurations, custom agents, scheduled tasks, and scripts

Read that as a hierarchy of how late you find out.

**A chat window** tells you immediately. The model picker shows a new name, you shrug, you carry on.

**A saved setting** tells you the next time you open that thing, which might be a month.

**A scheduled task** tells you nothing. It runs at 6am, fails, and sits there failing until somebody notices the report stopped arriving. If you built something clever with a pinned model six months ago, that is the thing that breaks, and it breaks quietly.

**A script in someone else's repository** may never tell you at all.

The asymmetry is the point. The cost of a retirement is not proportional to how much you used the model. It is proportional to how automated your use of it was.

## What to replace GPT-5.5 with

OpenAI's recommendations, verbatim from their docs:

| Your plan | Replacement |
|---|---|
| Plus, Pro, Business, Enterprise, Edu | GPT-6 Sol (`gpt-6-sol`) |
| Free and Go | GPT-6 Luna (`gpt-6-luna`), desktop app |
| Complex work, where available | GPT-6.1 Sol (`gpt-6.1-sol`) |

If you are on a paid plan and doing anything involved, the docs point at GPT-6.1 Sol rather than GPT-6 Sol, which is the kind of detail that only appears if you read past the headline replacement.

## The difference between the two companies is larger than it looks

This is the part I did not expect to find, and it changes how I would plan around either vendor.

**Anthropic publishes a lifecycle.** Every model carries one of four states: Active, Legacy, Deprecated, Retired. The deprecation page lists every model, its current state, the date it was deprecated and its retirement date, including models that have not been deprecated yet, where the entry reads "not sooner than" a given date.

They also commit to a minimum:

> Anthropic notifies customers with active deployments for models with upcoming retirements, providing at least 60 days' notice before model retirement for publicly released models.

And they tell you how to audit yourself. Go to the Usage page in the Claude Console, click Export, and the CSV shows usage broken down by API key and model. That is a direct answer to "am I still calling something that is about to die", which is the only question that matters here.

They have also committed to preserving the weights of retired models, with a public note on why retirement carries real costs for researchers and for anyone who valued a specific model.

**OpenAI publishes dates.** The retirement dates are in the documentation, clearly, with replacements named. What is not there is a stated minimum notice period or a single table showing the lifecycle state of everything at once.

Neither approach is wrong. But if you are deciding where to build something that has to keep running unattended, "at least 60 days and here is a CSV of your exposure" is a materially different proposition from "check the docs".

## What to actually do this week

**If you use ChatGPT casually:** nothing. You will see a different name on Wednesday.

**If you have a saved default or a workspace setting:** change it now rather than Wednesday. There is no benefit to waiting and the change takes seconds.

**If you have anything scheduled, automated, or in a script:** this is your week. Search your own configuration for the string `gpt-5.5` and anywhere a model is named. The ones that catch people are custom agents set up months ago and scheduled tasks that have been quietly working ever since.

**If you build on Anthropic:** run the usage export. Console, Usage, Export, read the CSV. Claude Sonnet 4.5 retires on 30 November and you have the tooling to find out in two minutes whether that affects you.

**If you are choosing a provider for something long-lived:** read their deprecation policy before their benchmark scores. One of these companies will tell you 60 days ahead and show you your own exposure. That is worth more than a few points on an evaluation.

## Why this keeps happening

Anthropic is unusually direct about the reason, which I appreciated:

> Anthropic currently deprecates and retires models to ensure capacity for new model releases.

That is the whole thing. GPUs are finite, every serving model occupies some, and a new release needs that capacity to come from somewhere. The same page acknowledges the cost: users who valued a specific model have to migrate, researchers lose access for comparative work, and there are safety and model welfare risks in retirement.

It is a capacity decision, not a product one, which is why it will keep happening regardless of how much people liked the model.

The practical consequence for anyone building on these tools is simple and slightly uncomfortable. **A model is not a dependency you pin once.** It is closer to a hosted service with an end date you were told about in a document you did not read.
