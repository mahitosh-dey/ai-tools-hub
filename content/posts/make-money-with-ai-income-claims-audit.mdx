One article in this set publishes more than sixty income figures and tells you it draws on "real-world data from successful AI freelancers and platform analytics." It names no survey, no platform, and no dataset. There is not a single link.

That is not the worst article here. Eight of the fifteen cite nothing whatsoever for any income figure they print.

## What I actually did

I took the page-one results for four variations of "make money with AI", deduped them to 20 distinct articles, and read each one with the same question: list every income figure on the page, and say whether a source is named at the point of the claim.

"At the point of the claim" is the whole test. A page that cites Statista for market size and then invents its own earnings ranges has not sourced its earnings ranges. A source counted only if it sat in the same sentence or paragraph as the number: a hyperlink, a named survey, a named report, or named company data.

Of the 20 URLs, I could read 15. Two are dead. Three blocked automated reading, and a 403 describes a bot policy rather than a dead page, so those three exist and I cannot audit them. I did not swap in replacements to reach a round twenty.

## Eight articles, 150 numbers, nothing behind any of them

The eight zero-citation articles are not fringe pages. They rank. Between them they print more than 150 income figures with no survey, report, platform dataset, or link attached to any of them.

The pattern is consistent enough to be a format rather than a lapse. A method gets a heading, the heading gets a dollar range, and the range arrives with the confidence of a measurement and the provenance of a guess.

The clearest example is `localaimaster.com`, which prints more than sixty figures and includes the line that 85 percent of students earn their first 100 dollars within seven days. That is a precise claim about a specific cohort with a specific deadline, and no cohort, no sample size, and no measurement window appears anywhere on the page.

`theinterviewguys.com` is the near miss. It names nine external sources and cites not one of them for an income figure. The sourcing apparatus is there. It is pointed at everything except the numbers readers came for.

## The best-sourced article is a web host selling hosting

DreamHost cites something for 18 of its 20 income figures. It was the only article in the set that did.

Its sources are not strong. They include ZipRecruiter job postings, a Udemy instructor forum post, PayScale data from 2022, and a 2021 Business Insider survey of twelve writers. Twelve writers in 2021 is thin evidence about AI-assisted income in 2026, and ZipRecruiter advertises what employers offer rather than what anyone takes home.

The point is that I can say all of that. A weak named source can be checked and argued with. An unnamed one cannot be checked at all, and nine articles here give you nothing to check.

The inverse correlation is the finding I did not expect. The articles printing the most income figures cite the fewest sources. One figure and no source is a small claim poorly supported. Sixty figures and no source is a different kind of document.

## Not one article cites a rate survey

Zero of fifteen.

Published rate benchmarks exist. The Editorial Freelancers Association's 2026 chart is built from more than 1,100 members reporting their 2025 rates. HeyOrca's 2026 social media pricing benchmark surveyed almost 100 agencies and freelancers. Neither is perfect and neither is specific to AI work. Both are real surveys with stated sample sizes, freely readable, and published this year.

None of the fifteen articles cites either. The sources that do appear point to job postings, another blog, a forum post, and a company's own job listings.

That is the actual state of this niche. It is not that the data is hard to find. It is that a category built on income promises has not gone looking.

## The one statistic everyone cites is two years old

Three articles cite Upwork for the AI hourly premium. They give three different numbers.

| Article | Figure cited to Upwork |
|---|---|
| fast.io | 44 percent more per hour |
| naoma.ai | 40 percent more per hour |
| ironhack.com | No number given |

Upwork's current figure is about 34 percent. The 44 percent comes from Upwork's Q4 2024 earnings call. Axios described the 2025 study as over 40 percent.

So the single most-repeated statistic in this niche is circulating in three versions, the highest of which is roughly two years stale, and the gap between the loudest version and the current one is ten percentage points. Nobody rechecked it. I only caught it because I had cited the 34 percent figure in [one of my own posts](/blog/how-to-use-chatgpt-to-make-money-online) four days earlier and recognised that 44 did not match.

## The most honest article has no numbers in it

Ironhack publishes no income figure at all. It says AI-skilled freelancers earn more per hour, names Upwork, and declines to put a range on it.

By doing less it became the only article in the set that does not overstate what is known. Monexa also prints no figures, though it reads more like it ran out of content than like it made a decision.

Ironhack sells a bootcamp. It had every commercial reason to print a number and did not.

## My own posts had the same defect

Two of mine carried monthly ranges under headings that read "Realistic income". Nothing about those figures was realistic in the sense of measured. They were my read of the market printed in the font of a finding.

I fixed both on 29 October 2026, before writing this. The headings now read "Reported range (estimate)". Where published data exists the figures are anchored to it, and where it does not the posts say so outright. Both carry dated correction notes.

One correction went up rather than down. My social media management figure of 300 to 800 dollars a month was low. HeyOrca's survey found only 31 percent of agencies and freelancers charging under 1,000 dollars a month, so I had been pitching my own estimate at the bottom of a market I had not checked. The error in this category runs in both directions, which is what you would expect from guesses rather than from bias.

My posts are not in the audited fifteen. Auditing yourself inside your own sample is not a method.

## The thirty second check

When you hit an income figure anywhere, including here:

**Look in the same paragraph for a name.** Not "studies show" or "data suggests" or "top freelancers report". A survey, a report, or a company with a dataset. Eleven of these fifteen articles fail at this step.

**Check the date on the source.** The 44 percent figure above was cited with no year attached in an article published this year. Two years is a long time in this market.

**Ask what the number measures.** Job postings are not earnings. Listing prices are not take-home. Project rates are not monthly income. Several of the sourced figures in this audit are real numbers measuring something other than what the sentence claims.

**Check who benefits.** If the figure is the promise of the product on the same page, weight it accordingly.

Three of those four take seconds. The fourth takes a glance at the top of the page.
