I pay for ChatGPT out of my own pocket and use it most days. This review has been revised twice now, and the November pass turned up the thing the August pass deliberately refused to print.

## The rumour I would not repeat is now on OpenAI's own page

In August I found a claim going round that ChatGPT Go carried advertising. I could not source it to OpenAI, only to blogs quoting each other, so I wrote that I would not repeat it as fact and left it out.

Read OpenAI's pricing page today and the Go plan card says it plainly: this plan may include ads.

I am pleased about the refusal and not about the outcome. Declining to print something you cannot source is only worth doing if you come back when the evidence turns up, so here it is, and it changes the advice at the bottom of this review rather than sitting in a footnote.

The scope is wider than Go. Axios and MacRumors both reported a pilot going live on February 9, 2026 across the Free and Go tiers in the United States, with Plus, Pro, Business, Enterprise and Education excluded. I am keeping those two sourcings separate on purpose. The Go line comes from OpenAI. The Free tier comes from the press. The first is a vendor describing its own product and the second is journalists describing it, and a review that blurs the two is doing the thing I complained about in August.

What it means in practice: $20 is now the price of not being advertised to. That is a strange sentence to write about a product people buy for its intelligence, and it is the single most useful fact on this page for anyone choosing between Go and Plus.

## The deadlines in this review have passed

The old version of this review said the $20 was justified by "memory, Canvas, higher rate limits, and DALL-E access."

It then described DALL-E's retirement as a thing about to happen. It has happened. The dall-e-2 and dall-e-3 models hit their shutdown on May 12, 2026 and now sit under past deprecations on OpenAI's developer site, and the August 30, 2026 retirement OpenAI set for the DALL-E GPT inside ChatGPT is also behind us. Image generation runs on the gpt-image family.

Your images did not stop working. The name you were sold did. The reason this is worth a section rather than a quiet edit is that a review written in the future tense about a past date is a specific kind of broken, and this one was broken that way for eleven weeks before I caught it.

## What this review got wrong about Claude

Two claims, both stated as fact, both false.

It said Claude Pro was "weaker for images, no voice mode, and no live web browsing by default." Claude has voice mode, on every plan including the free one, with continuous listening and connected tools it can use while you talk.

It also said that if Anthropic shipped cross-chat memory in Claude "that will be a real challenge to Plus. As of mid-2026 they still have not." Anthropic lists memory as a free tier feature on its own pricing page.

I checked those against Anthropic's pages rather than against my memory of them, which is what I should have done the first time. The [Claude review](/blog/claude-ai-review) now carries the corrected version, and if you want the two products side by side, [Claude vs ChatGPT](/blog/claude-vs-chatgpt) is the direct comparison.

## $20 is the middle of six price points

This is the change that matters most for anyone deciding what to pay, and it is the reason I retitled this review.

When I first wrote it, ChatGPT had one sensible paid tier for individuals. Count the options an individual can actually buy today and you get six: Free, Go, Plus, and three separate Pro tiers. Business, Enterprise and a separate Higher Education plan sit alongside those for organisations.

The Pro split is the part most reviews still have wrong, including the August version of this one, which told you Pro came in two tiers at $100 and $200. OpenAI's pricing page describes Pro as offering your choice of three usage tiers, and names them Pro 100, Pro 200 and Pro 500. Reporting puts those at $100, $200 and $500 a month, with the top tier carrying the highest included usage. So the ceiling is not $200. It is two and a half times that.

So "is $20 worth it" has stopped being the right question. The question is which of six is right, and for a decent number of people it is not the $20 one. If you use ChatGPT a few times a week and keep bumping into free tier limits, Go at $8 is the cheaper answer, with the caveat that now matters: Go and Free are the two tiers OpenAI shows ads on, and Plus is the first one it does not.

There is also metering to understand. Some models are charged per message in credits rather than simply being included, with the heavier reasoning modes costing more per message than the standard ones. Anthropic has moved the same way, and now says so on its own pricing page, which lists usage credits as a Pro feature. Both companies sell you an allowance rather than a flat product, and both are harder to forecast a month ahead than they used to be.

## The reasoning models you read about are gone

If you have an older article open in another tab telling you to use o1 for hard problems, close it.

o3 left ChatGPT on August 26, 2026, closing a ninety day sunset OpenAI announced in May. GPT-4.5 went before it. The separate o-series is finished as a product line.

One distinction worth holding onto, because a lot of coverage runs the two together: leaving ChatGPT and leaving the API are different events with different dates. OpenAI's developer deprecations page still lists an o3 API shutdown of December 11, 2026, and still shows GPT-4o and GPT-4.1 running to January 14, 2027. Those are deadlines for people writing code, not for people opening the app. If you read somewhere that o3 is still available, that is probably why, and it does not put it back in your model picker.

What replaced it is better, and simpler to explain. Reasoning is a setting rather than a different model you switch to. You choose how hard you want it to think, it spends longer before answering, and you get the step by step working. The behaviour I used to praise o1 for is still there. It just is not called o1 any more.

The family moved on again since August. This review used to describe reasoning as a setting inside the GPT-5 family, and OpenAI's deprecations page now lists a GPT-6 line as the replacement for most of what GPT-5 was.

I do not have to be vague about what that means per tier, because I read OpenAI's own comparison table for a different piece on November 5 and wrote down what it said. Go is marked unavailable for GPT-6.1 Sol, GPT-6 Astra, GPT-6 Sol, GPT-5.6 Sol and GPT-5.6 Luna, all of which Plus gets, and Go is left with GPT-6 Luna and GPT-5 Thinking Mini. There is a second wall one tier up that almost nobody mentions: GPT-5.6 Sol Pro is marked unavailable on Plus and available only on Pro. The full table across nine cheap plans is in [which models nine cheap AI plans actually run](/blog/cheap-ai-plan-model-limits).

So the model gap between Go and Plus is real and larger than the price gap suggests, and it is a better argument for the $20 than anything in the marketing. What I still will not do is tell you which exact version number sits on your plan today, because these names changed twice between my two readings and will change again. Open your model picker. That is more reliable than any review, including this one. For how the families compare with Anthropic's, I went through it in [GPT-5.6 vs Claude 5](/blog/gpt5-vs-claude4).

## How to choose a thinking level

Since reasoning became a setting rather than a separate model, the practical skill changed. You are no longer picking between products, you are deciding how much compute to spend on a question, and the wrong choice costs you either time or accuracy.

My own rule after a few months of this: use the standard mode by default, and raise the thinking level when a wrong answer would cost me more than the wait. Drafting an email, summarising a document, explaining a concept, standard mode is faster and just as good. Debugging something across several files, checking a piece of arithmetic that feeds into a decision, working through a contract clause, that is worth the extra thirty seconds.

The failure mode I see people fall into is leaving it on the heaviest setting permanently, on the theory that more thinking is always better. It is not. You burn your allowance faster, because heavier reasoning costs more per message, and on a lot of ordinary tasks the answer is identical. The other failure mode is never touching it and then concluding the model is bad at maths.

One thing genuinely worth the upgrade: you can read the working. When the answer is wrong, seeing where it went wrong tells you whether to re-prompt or start again. A confident wrong answer with no trace is far more expensive than a slow right one.

## The free tier and Go, honestly

Most reviews of Plus skip past the free tier in a sentence, which made sense in 2024 and does not now.

Free includes image generation and voice. It has memory. It searches the web. For someone who opens ChatGPT twice a week to draft something or check an idea, that is a working product, not a demo.

There are now two honest reasons to leave it rather than one. The first is the ceiling: you hit a cap, get moved to a lighter model, and notice the drop mid-task. The second is new since I last revised this review. Free carries advertising, according to the February reporting, and Go carries it according to OpenAI. The press account says you can opt out in exchange for fewer daily messages, which is a trade rather than an escape.

Go at $8 sits in the gap that used to be empty. More messages and more tool use than Free, longer memory, voice on the same terms. What it does not give you is the top of the model range, the full set of newer features, or a plan without ads on it.

Here is the test I would actually apply. Spend a fortnight on Free and pay attention to three things now: how often you hit a limit, whether you hit it while doing something that mattered, and whether the advertising bothers you. If none of the three bites, stay. If you hit limits occasionally and the ads do not trouble you, Go is $8. If you hit them in the middle of real work, or you find you resent being advertised to inside a tool you think with, that is the $20 signal, and you will know it without a comparison chart.

The thing nobody tells you is that moving down is fine too. If you paid for Plus during a busy quarter and your usage has dropped, Go now exists as a landing spot that did not before. Nothing punishes you for stepping down.

## What Plus actually buys you

Stripped of the marketing, here is the honest list.

Much more usage, and the better models rather than being bumped to a lighter one mid-task. This is the thing you feel, and it is most of the value.

Image generation in the chat, without switching tools. Still capped, still generous for normal work.

Voice, with real back and forth conversation and interruption. I use it while cooking and driving and almost never at my desk, which I suspect is true of most people who claim it is essential.

Memory across conversations. I dismissed this at launch and I was wrong. Not re-explaining who I am and how I write at the start of every session saves more time than any single model upgrade did.

Canvas, which opens a document beside the chat instead of dumping two thousand words into the message window. This is where I do long drafts now.

Projects, which group chats with their own instructions and files, so a new conversation starts already knowing the context.

Custom GPTs, plus the largest third party ecosystem of any assistant. If you rely on community built tools, this is the strongest reason to be on ChatGPT rather than Claude.

Priority access when things are busy.

No advertising. I would not have listed this a year ago because it was not a differentiator. It is one now, and of everything on this list it is the one most likely to decide the Go versus Plus question for someone.

## Where it still falls short

Hallucination has not been solved and will not be. ChatGPT states wrong things with complete confidence, and the better the writing gets the more convincing the wrong answers become. Web search reduces it and does not fix it. Verify anything that matters. That includes prices in this review, which is why I have dated them.

Quality drifts in very long conversations. Past roughly forty or fifty turns on a complex piece of work, I notice it losing the thread. Starting a fresh chat with a summary fixes it, and I have stopped treating that as a bug and started treating it as housekeeping.

Buying it is genuinely confusing now. Six tiers, credit metering, per message costs that differ by reasoning level, and model names that change every few months. I could not tell you what a heavy month costs without doing arithmetic, and that is a fair criticism of a consumer product.

For sustained code work I still reach for something else. [Claude](/blog/claude-ai-review) holds a detailed spec more tightly, and if you want the assistant inside your editor rather than a chat window, [Cursor vs GitHub Copilot](/blog/cursor-vs-github-copilot) covers what those actually cost once agent usage is metered.

It rewards people who know what they want. Give it a vague brief and you get something that looks competent and says nothing. That is not a flaw in the model so much as a description of what it is for.

chatgpt.com/pricing did return a page this time, which is an improvement on August. But the dollar amounts did not come through in the text. So I can tell you what OpenAI's page says the structure is, in its own words, and I cannot tell you from that page what anything costs.

That split runs through this whole review. The structure is first hand: six individual options, three Pro usage tiers named Pro 100, Pro 200 and Pro 500, Business starting at two users, and the line about Go possibly including ads. The prices are second hand, consistent across a lot of sources but not one of them OpenAI.

One thing did come through readable, on a different pass. When I went through OpenAI's pricing page on November 5 for a piece on cheap plan model limits, the per-model comparison table rendered even though the prices did not. That is where the GPT-6 tier detail above comes from, and it is first hand.

Of the two rumours I declined to print in August, one has resolved. Go advertising is now on OpenAI's pricing page, so it appears in this review as fact. The other, that the $200 Pro tier carries a one million token context window, I still have not seen from OpenAI. It stays out. Three months of it circulating is not evidence, and the whole point of the first refusal was that waiting is cheap.

## Which tier is actually right for you

Free is enough if you use it occasionally and can live with the caps and the advertising. It includes image generation and voice, so it is a real product rather than a trial.

Go at $8 is the tier most people have not heard of and some should be on. Occasional but regular use, annoyed by free limits, not willing to spend $20, not bothered by ads.

Plus at $20 is right if ChatGPT is part of most working days. Writers, developers, researchers, anyone generating images as part of the job. It is also where the advertising stops, which for some people will decide it on its own.

Pro 100 is the sensible first step for people who exhaust Plus daily. Before the tiers split, the only option above Plus was the $200 one, which was a bad deal for a lot of the people who took it.

Pro 200 and Pro 500 are for running ChatGPT constantly as core infrastructure, with Pro 500 reported as the highest included usage OpenAI sells. If you are choosing between them on anything other than having actually run out at the tier below, you are guessing.

Business matters for one reason more than any feature: it does not train on your content by default. If you handle client work, that is worth more than the admin controls.

## Alternatives at the same money

[Claude Pro](/blog/claude-ai-review) at $20 is the closest competitor and the gap is smaller than it was. Better at long form writing and holding a brief. Weaker for image generation and a smaller ecosystem.

Gemini at around the same price is worth a look if you live in Google Workspace, where the document and mail integration does real work. [ChatGPT vs Gemini](/blog/chatgpt-vs-google-gemini) goes through where the difference actually shows.

[Perplexity Pro](/blog/perplexity-ai-review) at $20 is the better research tool and a worse general assistant. It cites sources for every claim, which for factual work is a different way of using AI rather than a better version of the same thing.

Plenty of people pay for two. ChatGPT plus Claude at $40 total is the common pair, and I run it myself. If you can only pick one, pick on your dominant task rather than on feature counts.

## My verdict

ChatGPT Plus is still a good subscription and I am still paying for it. The models got better, memory and Canvas changed how I work, and the free tier improving does not bother me because it brings more people to the point where paying makes sense.

What changed is that Plus is one option among six, and the shopping is harder than the product. I cannot predict a month's cost without arithmetic, the model names move constantly, and the image generator readers were once told to pay for finished its retirement back in August.

Rating: 8 out of 10, unchanged from the August revision, and I want to explain why it did not move, because two things pulled in opposite directions.

Advertising arriving on Free and Go should have cost ChatGPT something. It did not cost Plus anything, because Plus is on the right side of that line. If anything it made the $20 easier to argue for: the gap between Go and Plus used to be a question of message limits, which is hard to feel in advance, and is now also a question of whether a tool you think with shows you ads, which is easy to feel. That is an uncomfortable reason to recommend a product and it is still a real one.

Pulling the other way, the ceiling moved from $200 to $500 and the shopping got worse again. There is now a genuine risk of paying $20 when $8 would do, or $500 when $100 would do, and six individual price points is more than a consumer product should need.

The product did not get worse. Buying it got more confusing and slightly more coercive at the bottom. Those cancel at 8.
