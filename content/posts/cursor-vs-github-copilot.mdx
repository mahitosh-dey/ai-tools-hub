For two years the shorthand for this comparison was $10 against $20. Copilot cheap, Cursor dear, pick based on whether multi-file editing was worth the difference. That comparison is now unbuyable, because Cursor retired the $20 plan and rebuilt its ladder from the bottom.

What replaced it inverts the old answer. Cursor's entry plan is now $8, which undercuts Copilot. But it cannot run Claude, GPT or Gemini at all, so the cheapest way to get a frontier model inside an editor is now Copilot, not Cursor.

Then I went looking at what Copilot Pro actually includes and found the same shape of problem on the other side. GitHub's own docs mark the Opus models as not included on Pro. You get Sonnet and Haiku at $10; Opus starts at $39.

So the useful version of this comparison is no longer which is cheaper. It is that the cheap plan on both products is missing a model you may specifically want, in two different ways, and neither vendor puts that on the price card. Every figure below came off the vendors' own pages, most recently on November 5, 2026. Where I could not verify something, I say so instead of repeating what the other results say.

## What GitHub Copilot actually costs now

On June 1, 2026, GitHub retired premium request units and replaced them with GitHub AI Credits. One credit is one cent. Usage is metered on actual token consumption, counting input, output and cached tokens against each model's published API rate, rather than counted as requests.

Then GitHub did something that has confused nearly everyone writing about this. It split each plan's allowance into two parts: base credits equal to the subscription price, and a flex allotment on top that GitHub adjusts over time as model prices move.

| Plan | Price | Base credits | Flex | Total monthly credits |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Free | $0 | None | None | 2,000 completions, limited chat and agents |
| Pro | $10 | $10 | $5 | $15 |
| Pro+ | $39 | $39 | $31 | $70 |
| Max | $100 | $100 | $100 | $200 |

Base credits get spent first. Once you pass them the flex allotment applies automatically at the same rates, whether you are working in your IDE, on github.com or in the CLI.

This is where the stale numbers come from. GitHub's own launch announcement says Copilot Pro is "$10/month, including $10 in monthly AI Credits." That post is still live, still ranks, and no longer matches the product. The live plans page says $15. The Max plan does not appear in the announcement at all, because it launched alongside the change rather than being described by it. If you have read three articles this month saying Copilot Pro gives you $10 of credits, that is why.

For teams, this post spent three months telling you to go and ask GitHub, because the marketing page had replaced both figures with a contact sales link and the only numbers available came from a launch announcement. That caveat can come off. GitHub's subscription-plans documentation now publishes them: Business at $19 per granted seat per month with 1,900 credits, Enterprise at $39 per seat with 3,900. Both work out to credits equal to the price, the same pattern as the individual base allowance. The marketing page still shows no figure, so if you look there and find nothing, the docs are where the number lives.

Now the part that does not appear on the price card at all. **Copilot Pro does not include the Opus models.** GitHub's model table marks Claude Opus 4.8, Opus 5, Opus 5.5 and Fable 5 as not included on Pro, and they start at Pro+ for $39. What $10 does buy is Sonnet and Haiku, currently Claude Sonnet 4.6 and Sonnet 5 alongside Haiku 4.5 and 5.5, plus the GPT and Gemini line-ups. For most work that is the right model anyway and you will never notice the ceiling. If you specifically came for Opus because something you read benchmarked it, $10 is not the plan that gets it, and nothing in the checkout flow will tell you so.

## What Cursor actually costs now

Every plan this post used to compare is now a legacy plan. Hobby, Start, Pro at $20, Pro+ at $60, Ultra and the Teams Standard seat all still work if you are on one, with their old two-pool billing intact, but none of them is sold any more. Cursor rebuilt the ladder and now sells it through what its docs call The X Subscription, one subscription covering Grok, Cursor and X.

| Plan | Monthly | Included usage | Claude, GPT and Gemini |
|---|---|---|---|
| Free | $0 | Limited | Not available |
| Plus | $8 | Base | Not available |
| Premium | $30 | 4x Plus | API list price, from prepaid credit |
| Super | $100 | 15x Plus | API list price, from prepaid credit |
| Ultra | $200 | 50x Plus | API list price, from prepaid credit |
| Teams | $30, $100 or $200 per seat | Matches the seat's tier | Same as the matching tier |
| Enterprise | Custom | Pooled | SCIM, audit logs, invoice billing |

Annual billing knocks a chunk off: $80 a year for Plus, $300 for Premium, $1,000 for Super and $2,000 for Ultra.

Two things about that table matter more than the prices. The first is the right-hand column. On Free and Plus, third-party models are not available at all. Cursor's docs put it plainly: Claude, GPT and Gemini are not part of the included pool, and on Premium and above they are available at the model's API list price, paid out of prepaid credit you buy separately. So Premium's $30 does not include any frontier-model usage. It includes the right to buy some.

The second is that the multiples are multiples of a number Cursor does not publish. Premium is 4x Plus, Super is 15x, Ultra is 50x. Plus itself is described only as "Base". The pool refreshes weekly rather than monthly and is shared across Grok and Composer. I cannot tell you what four times Plus is in dollars or tokens, because the thing being multiplied has never been stated, and I am not going to estimate it and present the estimate as a figure.

What $8 actually buys is narrower than the price suggests and worth spelling out, because the checkout does not. Plus gives you Cursor's own models, listed as Grok 4.7, 4.6, 4.5 and Composer 2.5, and it runs them at fixed settings: Fast off, effort medium, Grok 4.7 held to its 256k context window. No Auto, no Cloud Agents, no Automations, no Bugbot, and no bringing your own API key. It is a good plan for someone whose daily work Composer already handles. It is the wrong plan for anyone who picked Cursor in order to run Claude in it.

Auto deserves a note because it is the fact most out of date across the web, and it was wrong in my own [Cursor review](/blog/cursor-ai-review-2026) until I corrected it on July 31. Through 2025 Auto was genuinely free and became the standard advice for dodging overages. It then became metered. It is now not on Plus at all. If you are reading older advice that says to leave Auto on and stop worrying about the bill, that advice is two product generations stale.

## The cheap plan on both is missing a model

Here is the thing neither price card tells you, and the reason I retitled this post.

On Cursor Plus at $8, Claude, GPT and Gemini are not available at any price. Not metered, not capped, not behind an overage. Absent. You get Grok and Composer at fixed settings. To reach a third-party model at all you move to Premium at $30, and then you buy the usage separately on top.

On Copilot Pro at $10, frontier models are included, but not all of them. GitHub's model table marks Claude Opus 4.8, Opus 5, Opus 5.5 and Fable 5 as not included on Pro. Sonnet and Haiku are. Opus starts at Pro+, which is $39.

So both vendors have built a cheap tier with a hole in it, and the holes are different shapes. Cursor's is wide and obvious once you know to look: no third-party models at all. Copilot's is narrow and easy to miss: every frontier family is there, but the most capable member of one of them is not.

This matters because of how people choose. A developer reads a benchmark post, sees Opus or Sonnet win something, and buys the cheapest plan at the vendor they liked the look of. On Cursor that buys them an editor that cannot run the model they came for. On Copilot it buys them most of the way, until the day they specifically want Opus.

The fix is not complicated, it is just not something either checkout flow will do for you. Decide which model you actually want before you pick a price. If the answer is Opus, your cheapest route is Copilot Pro+ at $39. If the answer is Sonnet or GPT or Gemini, it is Copilot Pro at $10. If the answer is "whatever is fast and cheap and I do not want to think about it", it is Cursor Plus at $8, and you should go in knowing Claude is not part of the deal.

There is one group for whom none of this applies, and it is larger than you would guess. That group is the subject of the next section, and if you turn out to be in it you can stop worrying about every number above.

## The comparison nobody runs: what is free on both

There is one more thing both vendors give away, and it changes who should care about any of the above.

GitHub states that code completions and next edit suggestions remain included on all plans and consume no AI Credits. Cursor gives unlimited tab completions on every paid plan. So on both tools, the thing you interact with a thousand times a day costs nothing beyond the subscription.

What you pay for, on both, is agent work. Chat, multi-file edits, code review, background agents, CLI runs.

So the honest comparison is not between two prices. It is between $15 of fully specified agent budget on Copilot Pro, spendable on Claude Sonnet, GPT or Gemini, and an unpublished weekly pool on Cursor Plus that can only be spent on Grok and Composer. One of those I can reason about. The other I cannot, and neither can you, because the size of the Plus pool has never been published.

That asymmetry is the whole argument now. GitHub tells you exactly what you get and caps which models you may spend it on. Cursor declines to tell you how much you get and caps which models exist for you at all. If you like knowing your numbers, Copilot. If Composer already does your daily work and you would rather not think about a meter, Plus at $8 is a genuine bargain and the cheapest capable agentic editor I know of.

## What a credit actually buys

Worth knowing before the arithmetic below: Anthropic's own cost documentation puts a working developer at $150 to $250 a month at API rates, against subscriptions priced at $8 and $10. That gap is the reason every product in this category rebuilt its billing in 2026, and I went through what each vendor actually sells you in [AI coding agent pricing](/blog/ai-coding-agent-pricing-2026).

Both vendors now bill on tokens, and that quietly broke every "how many requests do I get" answer written before this summer.

Under the old system Copilot sold premium request units, so one request cost one unit and you could count them. Since June 1 it counts input, output and cached tokens against each model's published rate. Cursor works the same way, drawing from your pool at the provider's API price.

The practical consequence is that identical actions cost wildly different amounts. Ask an agent to rename a variable in a small file and you spend very little. Ask it to refactor across a large repository and it reads thousands of lines as context before writing anything, and the same single request costs many times more. Context size is the cost driver now, not request count.

This is why I distrust any article that tells you a given subscription buys a specific number of requests. From my own usage I have seen frontier-model requests land somewhere between roughly four cents and twenty cents depending on the model and how much context came along, which puts Copilot Pro's $15 somewhere in the low hundreds of requests, and the same arithmetic on whatever prepaid credit you load onto Cursor Premium. That is an observation from my billing dashboard, not a vendor figure, and your number will differ based on repository size more than on anything else.

Neither company publishes a requests-per-dollar figure, and I think that is deliberate rather than sloppy. The honest version is that they cannot publish one, because the answer depends on your codebase.

## What happens when you run out

The two products handle exhaustion very differently, and Copilot's design has a trap in it worth knowing before it appears on an invoice.

Cursor changed its answer here, and the direction is worth noticing. The old plans let you keep going on demand at API rates and settle up in arrears, which is the design this post used to praise for never downgrading your requests. The new ladder is prepaid instead: third-party models on Premium and above come out of credit you buy in advance at the provider's list price. You cannot overspend what you have not bought. That is a harder stop than the old one, and whether it is better depends entirely on whether you would rather be interrupted or invoiced.

On Plus there is no equivalent question, because there is nothing to run out of in that sense. The weekly pool refreshes, and third-party models are not an option you can reach at any price without moving up a tier.

Copilot spends base credits first, then applies the flex allotment automatically at the same rates across your IDE, github.com and the CLI. After that, usage bills as overage. GitHub added spending controls on July 2, 2026, and how they behave depends on which kind of budget you set.

A user-level budget always enforces a hard stop. GitHub's docs say there is no option to allow usage to continue past it, so an individual developer who sets one cannot be billed beyond it. Set it to zero and Copilot simply stops consuming credits when the included allowance runs out.

Budgets at the organisation, enterprise or cost centre level work differently, and this is the part to watch. Those have a setting called "Stop usage when budget limit is reached", and GitHub's documentation states it is off by default, followed by the instruction to always enable it when creating a budget. A budget with that toggle off is an alert, not a limit. Charges keep accruing past it. If you are an engineering manager who set a team budget and assumed it capped anything, go and check the toggle.

When a limit does block someone, code completions and next edit suggestions keep working, since those consume no credits on any plan. The blocked user stays blocked until the next billing cycle resets consumption or an administrator raises the budget.

I have gone looking for an equivalent default cap on the individual side and did not find GitHub documenting one. Read that as: if you set nothing, nothing stops the meter.

## Where each one wins on capability

Pricing decides less than people think. Two structural facts decide more.

The first is editor coverage. Copilot installs into VS Code, Visual Studio, the JetBrains IDEs, Neovim and Xcode. Cursor is its own editor, forked from VS Code, so adopting it means switching editors. If your team writes Kotlin in IntelliJ or Swift in Xcode, the comparison is over before anyone opens a pricing page. This is the most practical difference between the two products and it routinely gets four sentences in articles that spend six paragraphs on benchmarks.

The second is how each tool handles work that spans many files. Cursor's Composer takes an instruction like "add rate limiting to every route in this directory" and returns one atomic diff across every file it touched. Copilot has closed distance here, but Cursor still does this better, and it is why developers who switched mostly stayed switched.

Everything else is closer than the marketing on either side suggests. Both handle single-file edits well. Both do inline chat competently. Both support MCP servers now.

I should say plainly what I could not confirm. One of the top-ranking comparison pages reports that Copilot solved 56 percent of 500 SWE-Bench Verified tasks against Cursor's 51.7 percent, with per-task timings to two decimal places. I could not trace those numbers to any published benchmark run, and I found other pages repeating them without a source. They may be real. I am not going to pass them along as though I checked them, and you should be wary of any comparison that quotes precise benchmark figures without linking the run.

## Who owns Cursor now

Anysphere agreed to a $60 billion all-stock acquisition by SpaceX, announced June 16, 2026, and **the deal completed on August 14, 2026**. SpaceX subsidiary X67 Inc. merged into Anysphere, leaving Cursor as a wholly owned SpaceX subsidiary inside a division called SpaceXAI. It has been reported as the largest acquisition of a venture-backed startup on record. SpaceX had secured an option to buy the company on April 21.

The headline $60 billion is a valuation at announcement rather than a fixed figure, because the share exchange ratio was set by the volume-weighted average closing price of SpaceX stock over the seven trading days before closing. Until today this section still carried a paragraph saying the deal had not closed, directly beneath the sentence saying it had. That contradiction sat on the page for almost three months. It is gone now, and it is a good argument for re-reading a whole section when you correct one line of it rather than patching the line you noticed.

It follows SpaceX's merger with xAI in February 2026, which explains why Grok sits as a first-party model inside Cursor alongside Composer. Cursor's model documentation now describes them as "jointly trained by Cursor and SpaceXAI".

For a solo developer this changes nothing this year. The editor has behaved identically through every week of this saga. For anyone choosing a tool their team will standardise on, it is a live question: your source code will be processed by a wholly owned subsidiary of a satellite manufacturer that merged with an AI lab earlier the same year, and billed through a subscription bundled with a social network. Some legal departments will not care. Some will care a great deal. Better to ask now than after the rollout.

Copilot's ownership story is boring by comparison, and boring is the selling point. Microsoft has been answering enterprise procurement questions for thirty years and has the compliance paperwork to prove it.

Cursor is not the only tool in this category that changed hands. Windsurf, the alternative most lists still name, was absorbed by Cognition and no longer exists under that name. I checked which competitors are still shipping in [Cursor alternatives](/blog/cursor-alternatives-2026), and two of the most-recommended ones do not survive the check.

## How to check this yourself

This post will go stale. Everything in this category does, usually within about six weeks, which I know because I found five wrong prices in my own ElevenLabs review seven weeks after publishing it.

Three habits protect you. Read the vendor's live pricing page rather than its announcement blog, because the announcement is a snapshot of launch day and the page is current, which is exactly the trap the $10 credits figure fell into. Check whether an allowance sits behind the subscription, since the sticker price stopped being the cost when agents got metered. And check what your own checkout page offers, because regional pricing is now common enough that the dollar figure in any comparison article, including this one, may not be the number you are shown.

Sources first read on July 31, 2026, re-checked on August 12, October 5, and again on **November 5, 2026**. The November check is the one that forced this rewrite, and it did not find a wrong number so much as a wrong subject.

The ladder sold today is Free, Plus at $8, Premium at $30, Super at $100 and Ultra at $200, with Teams seats at $30, $100 or $200. The old title of this post promised a $10 against $20 comparison, and for several weeks one half of that comparison had not existed.

**Cursor Plus cannot run third-party models.** Cursor's docs state that Claude, GPT and Gemini are not part of the included pool, and that Plus and Free do not include them at all. On Premium and above they are available at the provider's API list price, from prepaid credit bought separately. This is the single most consequential thing on this page and it appears on no price card.

**Copilot Pro does not include Opus.** GitHub's model table marks Claude Opus 4.8, Opus 5, Opus 5.5 and Fable 5 as not included on Pro. They start at Pro+ for $39. Earlier versions of this post implied Pro gave you the frontier line-up without qualification. It gives you most of it.

So I can tell you with confidence that Pro, Pro+, Start and the rest are legacy and keep working as they are, because the docs say so directly. I cannot tell you what Cursor would move you to if you asked, because the page that would answer it is not currently reachable.
