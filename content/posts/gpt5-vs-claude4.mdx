OpenAI's top model and Anthropic's top model now cost exactly the same. Eight dollars per million input tokens, forty per million output, on both sides.

That is new, it is checkable on two vendor pages, and I have not seen anyone write it down. It also arrived while I was not looking: a week ago OpenAI's flagship was GPT-6 Astra at $10 and $50, which is what Anthropic's Fable 5.1 cost before they cut it to $8.

This post has been rewritten three times now, and each rewrite has found the same thing: the comparison moves faster than the article. So this version leads with what is true today, says when I read it, and spends its last section on how to check me.

## The quick version

Both vendors sell a family, not a flagship. The tier you buy matters more than the brand you buy it from, because a frontier tier from either beats a budget tier from the other.

**Anthropic** runs Haiku 5.5 for cheap fast work, Sonnet 5.5 for balance, Opus 5.5 for complex agentic coding, and Fable 5.2 above that for the hardest long-horizon reasoning.

**OpenAI** runs GPT-6 Luna for cost efficiency, GPT-6.1 Terra as the balanced everyday model, GPT-6.2 Sol above that, and GPT-6.2 Astra at the frontier. Their models page says it plainly: "If you're not sure where to start, use GPT-6.2 Astra, our flagship model for complex reasoning and coding."

## What each family costs

Read from Anthropic's models overview and OpenAI's developer pricing page on **23 November 2026**. Prices are per million tokens, input and output, at standard rates.

| Anthropic | In | Out | Context |
|---|---|---|---|
| Claude Fable 5.2 | $8 | $40 | 1M |
| Claude Opus 5.5 | $4 | $20 | 1M |
| Claude Sonnet 5.5 | $2 | $10 | 1M |
| Claude Haiku 5.5 | from $0.10 | from $0.50 | 1M |

| OpenAI | In | Out | Context |
|---|---|---|---|
| GPT-6.2 Astra | $8 | $40 | 1.05M |
| GPT-6.2 Sol | $2 | $10 | 1.05M |
| GPT-6.1 Terra | $1 | $5 | 1.05M |
| GPT-6 Luna | $0.10 | $0.50 | 1.05M |

Look at those two tables side by side. The top rows match exactly. So do the $2 and $10 rows. So do the $0.10 and $0.50 rows.

I cannot tell you why, and I am not going to invent a reason. Neither vendor announced its pricing as a response to the other. What I can say is that the days of a meaningful price gap between equivalent tiers appear to be over, which removes one of the three or four reasons people used to pick a side.

One figure that has not moved: every Anthropic number above was identical when I checked seven days earlier. OpenAI's side gained a whole generation in that time.

## Two cliffs the tables do not show

Both vendors now charge more for long prompts, and they do it differently.

**OpenAI charges by context length across the lineup.** Their page states it as "Short context: ≤272K input tokens. Long context: >272K input tokens." Above that line GPT-6.2 Astra goes from $8 and $40 to $16 and $60. GPT-6.2 Sol goes from $2 and $10 to $4 and $15.

**Anthropic charges by prompt length on the cheap tier only.** Haiku 5.5 is $0.10 and $0.50 up to a 100,000 token prompt, and $0.50 and $2.50 above it. Five times more, same model. Every other current Claude model carries the full 1 million token window at standard pricing.

That is what the word "from" is doing in the Haiku row, and it is the single most misquoted figure in this comparison right now. Haiku is a ten cent model for short work and a fifty cent model for long work.

## A million tokens is not what it was

The context number is the specification people quote most and understand least.

Anthropic's own page now puts 1 million tokens at **roughly 555,000 words**. It used to say about 750,000, and the page explains why: a newer tokenizer arrived with Claude Opus 4.7 and it produces more tokens for the same text.

Earlier versions of this post said 700,000 words. That was wrong by about a quarter, and it was wrong in the direction that makes the window sound more useful than it is. Same window, less text inside it.

The practical shape of the question has not changed. You need a very large window when the task requires holding one whole thing and reasoning across all of it: find every call site before renaming a function, check whether clause 14 contradicts anything in the appendices, trace one character's dialogue across a manuscript. Chunking those does not merely slow them down, it changes the answer, because the contradiction you are looking for lives between the chunks.

You do not need it for most of what people actually do. Drafting, summarising one document, answering a question, writing a function. Those fit in a fraction of the window, and paying for capacity you never fill is just paying more.

Two caveats comparison tables leave out. Large context is not free, because you are billed on tokens read, so a 900,000 token conversation costs roughly what 900,000 tokens costs on every turn. And a big window is not the same as good recall across it, which is an engineering problem rather than a marketing number, and one to test on your own material.

## How to pick a tier

Four questions, and none of them is "which model is smarter."

**How much must the model hold at once?** If the answer is a whole repository or a whole case file, every current model from both vendors now clears 1 million tokens, so this question no longer settles the tier the way it did a year ago. What it does settle is whether you are about to cross a pricing cliff.

**Is the work agentic or single-shot?** A single request that returns an answer behaves predictably and a cheaper tier usually handles it. An agent that runs twenty minutes, reads a hundred files, calls six tools and self-corrects is a different workload, which is why both vendors position specific tiers at it.

**How many times will you call it?** At a hundred calls a month the price difference is rounding. At ten million it is the whole business model, and the gap between $0.10 and $8 per million input tokens decides whether the product works at all. That gap is eighty-fold now, wider than it has ever been, because the cheap tier fell further than the frontier did.

**Is anyone waiting?** Frontier tiers think before answering, sometimes for a long time. That is right for an overnight refactor and wrong for a chat widget where someone watches a spinner. If latency binds, drop a tier rather than switching vendor.

## Why there is still no benchmark table here

You came for a scoreboard. I am not giving you one, and the reason is worth more than the scoreboard would be.

Specific head-to-head numbers circulate for these models, quoted to one decimal place, appearing across a dozen pages. I cannot trace a single one to a published benchmark run. Every page carrying them is an aggregator and several quote each other, which is how a number acquires the look of consensus without anyone having measured anything.

There is a second reason now, and this refresh supplied it. The figures circulating in September were for Claude Opus 5 against GPT-5.6 Sol. Both models have since been superseded, OpenAI's twice over. The numbers were unsourced and they are now unsourced and obsolete, still being quoted as current.

If you want a number you can trust, run the same real task through both. That takes an afternoon and beats every table on the internet, including any I might write.

## Where each family still differs

Some differences are structural rather than a matter of degree.

**Image generation.** Claude reads images and PDFs but does not generate them. Anthropic's capability line on 23 November reads that all current models support text and image input, text output, multilingual capabilities, vision and tool use, with no image output counterpart. If generation is part of your workflow, that is ChatGPT or a specialist tool.

**Tier granularity at the bottom.** Both vendors now have a genuinely cheap tier at ten cents per million input tokens, which was not true six months ago. Anthropic's comes with a prompt-length cliff and OpenAI's does not, so for high-volume work on long inputs the shapes differ even though the headline matches.

**Ecosystem.** ChatGPT bundles more into the consumer product: voice, image generation, browsing, code interpreter, custom GPTs. Claude's consumer product is narrower and deeper on text and code. Different products at a similar price, not better and worse.

## What the consumer plans cost

Claude Pro is **$17 a month billed annually**, which Anthropic bills as $200 upfront, or **$20 monthly**. Above it sits Max at $100 and Ultra at $200. If you read about a Max 20x plan anywhere, that is now Ultra.

ChatGPT Plus is $20, Go is around $8, and Pro is sold at $100, $200 and $500 rather than as one tier.

## Which one should you get

| You are | Pick | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Writing or coding all day | Claude Pro | Text and code depth, 1M context, tiering up to Opus 5.5 when needed |
| Doing a bit of everything | ChatGPT Plus | Images, voice, browsing and code in one product |
| Building on an API at volume | Haiku 5.5 or GPT-6 Luna | Both ten cents per million in; watch Haiku's 100K prompt cliff |
| Running long agentic tasks | Opus 5.5, Fable 5.2 or GPT-6.2 Astra | All positioned for it, all at 1M context |
| Unsure, and it is for work | Both, about $40 total | Good at different things; not a real decision against a salary |

Pick on your dominant task rather than a leaderboard. If one subscription has to cover everything, use the other's free tier for the gaps. Which model that free tier actually gives you is its own question, and I went through five vendors' answers in [what the free tier does not tell you](/blog/which-model-free-tier-gives-you).

## My setup

Claude Pro as the daily driver, because most of my day is writing and code and the long context earns its keep on both. ChatGPT free alongside for image generation and the occasional voice conversation. For coding I work through an editor rather than a chat window, a separate decision I worked through in [Cursor vs GitHub Copilot](/blog/cursor-vs-github-copilot).

I have changed this setup twice in a year and expect to again. That is the actual lesson: nobody's stack should be load-bearing on a specific model being ahead.

## How to check this yourself

This post will go stale, and I can now tell you roughly how fast.

**GPT-6.2 appeared in OpenAI's developer documentation within seven days of my last check.** I read that page on 16 November and again on 23 November, and a new flagship had arrived in between. That is the honest clock on any comparison article, including this one.

**Watch for the name trap.** OpenAI has kept Sol, Terra and Luna across three GPT generations. Someone who checks "does this article still say Sol?" gets a yes and concludes it is current, when the article may be two generations out. Check the number in front of the name, not the name.

**Go to the vendor's own model or pricing page, not an article.** Both publish current lineups and rates. Both also keep old announcement posts online, which is how obsolete figures stay in circulation: the announcement is a snapshot of launch day while the pricing page is current.

**Be suspicious of precise benchmark numbers with no link to the run.** One decimal place is not evidence.

**And do not check the price to decide whether an article is current.** That is the check most people reach for, and it fails, because vendors keep selling old models at their old prices. I took ten articles ranking for the best coding model, and of the seven that loaded, six named a superseded model while every price they quoted was still accurate. The counting is in [six of seven ranking articles name a model that has been replaced](/blog/ai-model-articles-stale-audit).

**The URL of this page is historical.** It says gpt5-vs-claude4 because that is what the comparison was when it was written in June. I have left it alone deliberately rather than renaming it every few weeks, and the title tells you what it actually covers.
