Every AI detector sells you a percentage. Winston AI says 99.87 percent accuracy. GPTZero says 99.9 percent. Originality.ai says 99 percent and up. More usefully, three of them now publish a false positive rate, and all three land near 1 percent.

Those numbers all sound like the problem is solved. Then you multiply one of them by a class size, and it stops sounding that way.

That multiplication is the whole point of this post, because nearly every article about AI detectors either repeats the vendor percentage or repeats a claim about bias, and almost none of them do the arithmetic that turns a percentage into a number of people.

I read these pages on 18 August 2026 and read them again on 13 November. Almost every number had moved in the eighty-seven days between. That turned out to be a finding of its own, and there is a section on it below.

## The number that matters is not accuracy

Accuracy is the wrong headline figure, and it is the one every vendor leads with.

An AI detector can be wrong in two directions. It can miss AI text, which is a false negative, or it can flag human writing as AI, which is a false positive. Those two errors are not remotely equal in cost. A false negative means a piece of generated text slips through and nobody notices. A false positive means a person is told they cheated.

One of those is a missed catch. The other is an accusation with a name attached.

So when a detector advertises a single accuracy number, it is blending the error you care about with the error you mostly do not. The figure to ask for is the false positive rate, and most of these companies do not put it on the marketing page.

## What the vendors actually claim

Here is what each one publishes on its own site, read on 13 November 2026.

| Tool | Claimed accuracy | Publishes a false positive rate |
|---|---|---|
| GPTZero | 99.9 percent, 96.5 percent on mixed documents | Yes. No more than 1 percent, and 1 percent for ESL writing |
| Winston AI | 99.87 percent | Yes. Zero across 27,743 human documents, and a 1 percent condition in a third party test |
| ZeroGPT | 98.4 percent | Yes. Under 1 percent |
| Originality.ai | 99 percent and up on its current models | No number on any page I could read |
| Sapling | No figure published | No number, but says plainly that false positives will occur |
| Turnitin | Could not be read today, see below | 1.3 percent as read in August |

The accuracy column has quietly converged. Five of these six sit between 98.4 and 99.9 percent, which should make you more suspicious rather than less, because they are not testing the same thing.

The false positive column is where they still disagree, and it is the column that matters. Winston says zero false positives across 27,743 human documents. GPTZero says it keeps its rate at no more than 1 percent and makes no claim of zero. Those are the same task. One of those companies is describing a test it passed perfectly and the other is describing a ceiling it refuses to go above.

There is no shared test set behind any of this, so each company is measuring its own thing and calling it accuracy. Treat the percentages as marketing until somebody shows you the test.

## What changed in eighty-seven days

I read all of these pages on 18 August 2026 and again on 13 November. I was expecting to confirm a set of figures and change a word here and there. Almost nothing held.

| Tool | 18 August 2026 | 13 November 2026 |
|---|---|---|
| Winston AI | 99.98 percent, no false positive figure | 99.87 percent, and now publishes false positive data |
| GPTZero | 99 percent | 99.9 percent |
| ZeroGPT | No accuracy figure published at all | 98.4 percent, and an under 1 percent false positive rate |
| Originality.ai | 97.8 percent for its multilingual model | That model was sunset in October 2026 |
| Sapling | No figure, warns false positives will occur | Unchanged, word for word |
| Turnitin | Figures readable on the site | Returns 403 to automated requests |

Four of the six moved. Winston's headline accuracy went down, which is not the direction marketing numbers usually travel and is to their credit. GPTZero's went up. ZeroGPT went from publishing nothing to publishing two figures, including a false positive rate better than the one GPTZero commits to. And the Originality.ai number this post quoted in August belongs to a model the company has since switched off.

None of these companies announced a change in how they measure. There was no new benchmark, no methodology note, no correction. The numbers simply read differently on a Thursday in November than they did on a Monday in August.

That is the argument of this post, and I did not have to construct it. I only had to write the figures down twice.

It also sets the shelf life on everything above. Any article quoting a detector's accuracy figure is quoting a reading from a particular day, whether it says so or not. This one says so.

## Now do the multiplication

Use 1 percent. Not because it is the worst case, but because it is the number the vendors themselves have settled on. GPTZero caps its false positive rate at 1 percent. ZeroGPT publishes under 1 percent. Winston's best third party result is measured at a 1 percent false positive condition. Three companies, three pages, one figure, and I can quote all three today.

A single essay at 1 percent feels like nothing. You would take that bet.

A class of 300 essays produces about three falsely flagged students.

A department running 5,000 submissions in a year produces about 50.

A university processing 50,000 submissions a year produces roughly 500 students whose honest work was flagged as machine written.

Set out as a table, because this is the part worth screenshotting.

| Documents scanned | Falsely flagged at 1 percent | Roughly |
|---|---|---|
| 1 essay | 0.01 | Nothing to worry about |
| 30, one class | 0.3 | Less than one a term |
| 300, a course | 3 | About three students |
| 5,000, a department | 50 | One a week |
| 50,000, a university | 500 | Three every two working days |

None of those students did anything wrong. The rate did not change. Only the denominator did, and the denominator is the part nobody puts on the pricing page.

The bottom row is the one to sit with. At a mid-sized university, on a rate the vendors advertise as a selling point, roughly 500 honest students a year are flagged. That is not a scandal or a bug report. It is the advertised performance of the product working exactly as specified.

The earlier version of this post ran the same table at Turnitin's 1.3 percent and got 650. Dropping to the industry's own preferred 1 percent takes 150 students off that list and leaves 500 on it. If the argument survives a third off the rate, the rate was never the weak part of it.

This is the same shape as every other number I have taken apart on this site. Runway's free tier is a real offer until you convert credits into seconds. Gamma's free plan is generous until you notice the meter runs per slide. A 1 percent error rate is negligible until you multiply it by how many times you run the test.

## A score is a probability, not a verdict

Every one of these tools returns a number, and almost everyone reads that number as an answer. It is not one.

I need to correct myself here, because the August version of this post got it wrong. It said Originality.ai describes a mixed score as the tool expressing uncertainty rather than identifying part of the document as machine written, and credited them for making a point against their own commercial interest.

Their pages say the opposite, in plain words. An AI detection score "shows the percentage of AI involvement in text", and the three categories are Likely AI, Mixed Writing, and Original. That is a claim about how much of the text is machine written, not a confidence interval. I have not found the sentence I attributed to them on any page I can reach, and I am not going to keep crediting a company for a position it does not hold.

What they do say, and it is worth having, is that "AI Detectors are NOT 100% accurate and never will be" and that scores "should be treated as a signal, and not proof of misconduct".

The underlying point still stands on its own without a vendor to lend it weight. A detector reports a statistical judgement, and that distinction collapses the moment the score reaches somebody without the training to read it. A number between 0 and 100 next to a student's name looks like a measurement. It gets treated like a breathalyser reading, when it is closer to a weather forecast.

There is a second trap in how the scores get used. If a teacher runs a document through three different detectors and acts on the highest score, they have not triangulated anything. They have selected for whichever tool was most wrong in the direction they were already leaning. Three tools with independent error rates produce more false positives between them than any one of them alone, not fewer.

If you take one operational rule from this post, make it this: one tool, one threshold, agreed in advance, and the score never travels without the word count beside it.

## The bias claim, and why I could not confirm it

I started this expecting to write that AI detectors are biased against non-native English speakers. It is the most repeated criticism of these tools, it spread from early research on older detectors, and it is stated as settled fact in a great many articles.

Turnitin's own published evaluation does not support it. They tested nearly 2,000 English Language Learner writing samples and report a 0.014 false positive rate for ELL writers against 0.013 for native English writers. That is not a meaningful gap.

One caveat on that evidence, added in November. Turnitin now returns a 403 to automated requests on its main site and on its guides and help subdomains, so I could not re-read those figures today. They are quoted here as I read them on 18 August 2026 and should be treated as a dated reading rather than a current one. A 403 describes a server policy, not a company in trouble, and nothing suggests the numbers have changed. I simply cannot stand behind them this week the way I could in August.

I want to be careful about how much weight that carries. This is a vendor publishing research about its own product, on its own website, alongside a citation to independent work in which it performs well. That is not the same as an independent replication, and you should discount it accordingly.

But it is specific, it names a sample size, and it is checkable. That puts it ahead of a bare accuracy percentage with no methodology attached. So my honest position is that the bias claim is unproven on current tools rather than established, and that anyone stating it flatly in 2026 is repeating something they have not checked.

GPTZero, for what it is worth, implicitly concedes the concern exists. Its own site still says it repeatedly retrains the model to reduce the false positive rate for ESL writing to 1 percent, and a separate page claims it is the only detector specifically de-biased for ESL writers. That is both an admission that the problem was real and a rate roughly seven times higher than the Turnitin figure above.

Read those two together and the honest summary is narrow. One vendor says it measured no gap. Another vendor says it had to engineer the gap away and will tell you the rate it settled at. Neither is independent replication.

## The 300 word threshold changes everything

This is the most practically useful thing I found, and it is buried.

Turnitin's published false positive figures apply to documents that meet a 300 word minimum. That is not a footnote. It means the reliability numbers simply do not describe shorter work. This also comes from the August reading, for the reason given above.

Sapling goes further, and this one I did re-read today. Its page still says the more essay-like a piece of writing is, the more likely it is to result in a false positive.

Put those together. Short answers, discussion board posts, single paragraph reflections and brief written responses are the least reliable case for these tools, and they are exactly the work that gets scanned in bulk because there is so much of it.

If you are a teacher, this gives you a defensible policy in one line: do not run detection on anything under 300 words, because the vendor's own numbers do not cover it.

## The most honest page any of them publish

Sapling's detector page includes this: false positives and false negatives will occur.

No percentage, no qualifier, no claim of being the most accurate detector on the market. It then explains that formal, essay-like writing is more likely to trigger a false positive, which is an admission directly against its own commercial interest.

That single sentence is more useful than every 99 percent claim in this post combined, because it tells you how to hold the output. Not as a verdict. As a probability that will sometimes be wrong in the expensive direction.

## What free AI detectors actually give you

Most of the well-known detectors run a free tier that caps how much text you can check at once, with the full document handling behind a paid plan. GPTZero, ZeroGPT, Sapling, Winston AI and Originality.ai all still operate and all responded when I checked them on 13 November 2026.

Three things worth knowing before you rely on any of them.

Copyleaks, Quillbot and Scribbr all block automated access, and Turnitin has now joined them. I could not read their current terms directly and I am not going to quote you numbers I could not verify.

Writer.com's dedicated AI content detector page still redirects to an anchor on its homepage rather than a page of its own. I flagged that in August as the kind of thing that happens shortly before a feature stops being a priority. Three months on, the redirect is still there and the page has not come back.

And Originality.ai has retired two products since August. Its own page states that Multi Language and AI Allowance were sunset in October 2026. If you picked a detector earlier this year on the strength of its multilingual accuracy, that is worth knowing.

## If you have been falsely accused

Do these in order.

Ask for the score and the word count. If the work is under 300 words, the vendor's published reliability figures do not apply to it, and you can say so specifically rather than vaguely.

Ask which tool produced the score and what false positive rate that vendor publishes. Many people running these tools have never seen the number.

Then show your process rather than arguing about the percentage. Version history in Google Docs or Word, intermediate drafts, notes, timestamps, browser history. A probability score cannot be disproved by insisting you did the work, but a document that visibly grew over eleven sessions is very hard to argue with.

That last point matters more than anything else here. Keep your drafts. Not because you are guilty, but because the tools have a known error rate and you may need to be the one who proves it.

## Why this is hard to fix

Detectors mostly work by measuring how predictable text is. Machine writing tends to sit in a narrower band of word choice and sentence rhythm than human writing does.

The problem is that some humans naturally write that way. Clear, formal, well-structured prose with conventional phrasing is exactly what a model produces, and it is also what students are taught to produce. Writing advice and detection triggers point in the same direction.

That is why the error will not go to zero, and why the vendor that says so plainly is the one telling you the truth. Every model gets better, then writing tools get better, and the gap narrows again from both sides. If you use AI in your own writing workflow, my [how to write a blog post faster with AI](/blog/write-blog-post-faster-with-ai) piece is about drafting with it rather than hiding it, which is the distinction that matters here.

## What I am not going to write

A large share of the search demand around this topic is people looking for ways to beat these tools. Bypass guides, humanizer services, one click removal of AI traces.

I am not writing that, and it is worth saying why rather than quietly leaving it out.

It helps the people least harmed by being caught, since somebody deliberately cheating has already accepted the risk. It does nothing for the person this post is actually for, which is the student flagged for writing their own essay. And it pushes institutions toward scanning harder, which raises the total number of false positives for everyone.

If you use a grammar tool heavily, that is worth knowing about for a different reason. Originality.ai still states, word for word, that tools which use AI to rewrite or edit copy, and it names some Grammarly features, can cause false positives. In August I wrote that as false positives "in other detectors". The source does not say that, so I have dropped it. My [Grammarly review](/blog/grammarly-review-2026) covers what those features do.

## The verdict

Two things to take away.

Ask for the false positive rate, not the accuracy rate. Accuracy blends the error nobody pays for with the error somebody pays for, and only one of those ends with a person in a misconduct meeting.

And do the multiplication yourself. At the 1 percent rate three of these vendors advertise, a university running 50,000 submissions a year is producing around 500 false flags annually, entirely predictably, as designed. Not as a malfunction. That is what the number means, and it is why a detector score should start a conversation rather than finish one.

One more, now that I have read these pages twice. Write down the date next to any figure you take from a vendor page, including the ones in this post. Four of the six numbers here changed in under three months, and not one of those companies told anybody.

If you are a student, keep your drafts. If you are a teacher, set a word floor and never let a score be the whole finding. Either way, the tool is a probability, and it has been telling you so in the small print all along.
