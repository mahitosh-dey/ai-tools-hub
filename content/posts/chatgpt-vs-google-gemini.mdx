Two years ago this comparison had a clear answer. Then it became genuinely close. Then the two products converged on the same commercial shape while keeping different things behind the paywall, which is where I left it in August.

What happened since is more interesting than another round of convergence. They have started trading places. OpenAI broke the price symmetry at the top, and Google quietly abandoned the thing I praised it for.

## The correction notice, again

In August I rewrote the Gemini half of this post because it described Gemini 2.0 Flash, Gemini 2.0 Pro, Gemini 1.5 Pro, Imagen 3, and a plan called Gemini Advanced. I called that a product generation roughly eighteen months old, presented as current.

Three months later the Gemini half had rotted again. It described Gemini 3.1 Pro, Flash and Flash-Lite as current. Google is now selling Gemini 4.

Worse, I got an argument wrong rather than just a name. This post told you that Google gives every tier the same models and that paying buys volume rather than capability. I called it the most underrated thing about Gemini. It is no longer true, and I know exactly when it stopped being true, because [I published a post about Google's free tier on 16 November](/blog/which-model-free-tier-gives-you) that documented the opposite from Google's own cards. For eight days this site argued both sides of the same question. The newer post was right.

I am keeping the retractions at the bottom rather than burying them, and the rest of this is rebuilt.

## They converged on price, then OpenAI broke it

Here is the ladder as it reads today.

| | OpenAI | Google |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Free | $0, may show ads | $0 |
| Cheap tier | ChatGPT Go, price unpublished | AI Plus, reported $4.99 |
| Mid tier | Plus, $20 | AI Pro, reported $19.99 |
| Upper tiers | Pro, $100 and $200 | Ultra 5x and 20x, reported $99.99 and $199.99 |
| Top tier | **Pro 500, $500** | **nothing** |
| Business | Business, per seat | Workspace tiers |

For about six months the interesting fact about these two companies was that their price points were the same numbers. That is still true in the middle and most of the way up. It stopped being true at the top, because OpenAI added a third Pro tier at $500 and Google has not answered it.

OpenAI's help centre is direct about the structure: choose from three monthly Pro plans, at $100, $200 and $500, billed monthly, with no annual option and no paying several months ahead. The tiers differ on access to the Ultrafast variants of its reasoning models. Pro 100 does not get them, Pro 200 gets one of them inside Codex only, and Pro 500 gets them.

So the shape of the argument I made in August has inverted. I said three companies converging on identical price points told you the pricing was a response to each other rather than to the cost of running the models. The $500 tier points the other way. It exists because a specific kind of very heavy user will pay it, and Google's consumer ladder stops at roughly $200 because Google's answer to that user is Workspace and Google Cloud rather than a bigger Gemini subscription.

If you want the tier-by-tier version of whether any of this is worth buying, that is in the [ChatGPT Plus review](/blog/chatgpt-plus-review).

## Neither company's pricing page prints a price

I need to explain where the figures above come from, because the sourcing is stranger than it was in August.

OpenAI's pricing page at chatgpt.com/pricing answers a request and lays out every plan, with the names, the taglines and the feature tables all present. It prints the words per month under each plan. It does not print a number next to them. The Pro card reads From, then per month, with the space between them empty.

Google's plan page does the same thing. The cards on one.google.com show per month with no figure, and the Ultra card says From per month with nothing in front of it.

Two companies, same month, same omission. I do not think it is coordination, and I am not going to tell you it is a dark pattern, because a page that renders prices from a regional service will show you blanks when that service does not answer for your request. What I can tell you is the practical effect: the pricing page is no longer where you find out what either product costs.

The prices that do exist live elsewhere. OpenAI's help centre still states Plus at $20 a month and the three Pro tiers at $100, $200 and $500, so those four figures are vendor-confirmed and I read them on 24 November. OpenAI does not publish a Go price on any page I could read, which is a strange gap for a plan it actively sells. Google's own subscriptions page served me rupees rather than dollars, at 399 for Plus, 1,950 for Pro, and 6,500 and 19,500 for the two Ultra variants, and printed no USD figure for any consumer plan. Every Google dollar figure in this post is third-party.

I have been tracking this pattern across vendors in [the pricing page audit](/blog/ai-pricing-page-vs-docs-audit), and it keeps getting worse rather than better.

## What each one gates, and how that flipped

This was the strongest section of the August version and it is the one that needs the most work, because the finding reversed.

**Google used to gate volume and now gates capability too.** In August, Google's help pages said every tier including free got the same three models, and paying bought usage. Today its plan pages tie models to tiers. Free is listed with Gemini 4 Hydrogen. AI Plus lists access to Hydrogen. AI Pro lists access to Gemini 4 Neon. Ultra lists the highest access to Gemini 4 Carbon, which Google's other plan page introduces with the line about unlocking our strongest model. A footnote adds that Carbon is available in selected countries only.

That is the same structure OpenAI has always used, arrived at from the opposite direction.

**Google's volume gating is still the clearer half.** Its subscriptions page states that your limit refreshes every five hours until you reach your weekly limit, which is a real mechanic you can plan around, and it is the same shape Anthropic uses for Claude. The multipliers are 2x on Plus and 4x on Pro measured against the free plan, then 5x and 20x on the two Ultra variants measured against AI Pro. Note that those two sets of multipliers have different baselines, which is easy to misread as 20x the free plan when it is 20x a plan that is already 4x the free plan.

**Both of them meter in credits now.** This is the other half of the flip. My August line was that Google publishes a simple multiplier while OpenAI meters some models per message in credits. Google now sells AI credits to Pro and Ultra subscribers for extra usage, and its help page says they are deducted based on standard API pricing for the specific model and complexity of request. It will not tell you what the increments cost. OpenAI's credits work on the same principle, spending your included allowance first and your credit balance after.

A metering system whose unit price depends on model and request complexity is not something a reader can budget against. Both companies now have one.

Worth noting for context: Anthropic is still stricter than either, and I compared that properly in [Claude vs ChatGPT](/blog/claude-vs-chatgpt).

## What Gemini actually is in November 2026

The family is Gemini 4. Carbon is the top model, Neon sits in the middle, and Hydrogen is the fast one at the bottom.

I want to be honest about how unstable this is, because I have a dated record of it on this site. On 16 November I read Google's subscriptions page and wrote down which model each plan card named. The slot now occupied by Neon said **Argon** eight days ago. [Gemini 4 Neon appears to have launched around 18 November](https://www.therundown.ai/news/google-gemini-4-neon-release), reported as Google's fourth Gemini 4 model in under two months, and it has taken Argon's place on the plan cards. Argon still exists; Google announced it separately and it went to cyber defenders first.

Both reads were from India on desktop, so that is a like-for-like comparison of the same page eight days apart, not a regional difference.

Google's pages do not agree with each other about this ladder either. On gemini.google the card footers run Free to Hydrogen, Plus to Neon, Pro to Carbon. On one.google.com they run Plus to Hydrogen, Pro to Neon, Ultra to Carbon. Those are different claims about what Plus includes. The same subscriptions page also still calls 3.1 Pro our leading model in one place while the other page sells Carbon as the strongest. I went through this card-by-card in [the free tier post](/blog/which-model-free-tier-gives-you), and the short version is that Google's own pages name different models for the same plan and never reconcile them.

Beyond the names, the paid tiers add real things. Omni Flash handles video generation across the tiers. Google Flow is the creative tool built around it, metered in credits at 200 a month on Plus, 1,000 on Pro, and 10,000 or 25,000 on the two Ultra variants. Storage runs 400GB, 5TB, 20TB and 30TB, which matters because it is bundled with the Google One storage many people already pay for. The paid tiers also carry Colab compute units and Google Cloud credits, which almost nobody buying a chat assistant will use and which pad the comparison tables nicely.

Three things still make Gemini genuinely different rather than just competitive.

**Search is native rather than bolted on.** When Gemini looks something up it is using Google Search as part of how it reasons, not calling a browsing tool. In practice it is better at knowing when to search and less likely to confuse recent events.

**Workspace integration.** Reading your Gmail threads, summarising Drive documents, working inside Docs and Sheets. This is the one advantage in this entire comparison that has not moved in a year, and OpenAI still has no answer to the mail and calendar half of it.

**Android.** Gemini replaced Google Assistant as the default, so it reaches your contacts, calendar and system apps. On iOS both are just apps and neither has an edge.

## What ChatGPT actually is in November 2026

Six tiers: Free, Go, Plus at $20, Pro at $100, $200 or $500, then Business and Enterprise.

The biggest change since August is not a model. **The free and cheap tiers now carry advertising.** OpenAI's help centre states that ads may appear for users on the Free and Go plans, and that Plus, Pro, Business, Enterprise and Edu accounts will not have ads. The Go card on the pricing page says the plan may include ads. Ad testing started in the United States on 9 February 2026, and availability is regional. Accounts identified as belonging to users under 18 are not shown ads.

There is an Ads-Free option on the Free plan, and the trade is worth understanding before you reach for it: it removes ads and comes with lower usage limits. Turning off personalisation makes ads less tailored without removing them. So on Free you choose between ads and a smaller allowance, and on Go the only route out is paying for Plus. I went through what that does to the value of each tier in the [ChatGPT Plus review](/blog/chatgpt-plus-review), and it is also why the [image generation comparison](/blog/midjourney-vs-dalle3) reads differently now than it did in June.

The model lineup has moved on again. The pricing page's model table runs through the GPT-6 family, with GPT-6.1 Sol, GPT-6 Astra and GPT-6 Sol on Plus and expanded on Pro, and GPT-6 Luna available down to Free. The help centre adds that GPT-6 Pro, powered by GPT-6 Astra, is available on Pro 100, Pro 200, Business and Enterprise. There is a small contradiction in OpenAI's own pages about what the free tier actually gets, with the pricing table marking GPT-6 Luna as available on Free while a help article says free and Go users receive GPT-5.6 Luna. I am not going to resolve it for them.

Usage on the free tier is described as unlimited everyday text chats subject to abuse-prevention safeguards, with separate limits still applying to file uploads, image generation, voice and data analysis. That is a more generous-sounding free tier than Google's, with advertising as the price.

The image generator story is unchanged from last week and worth restating because it is the single most out-of-date fact in other people's comparisons. The DALL-E API was shut down on 12 May 2026, the DALL-E GPT was retired on 30 August 2026, and the name is now simply absent from OpenAI's pricing and models pages rather than listed as deprecated. Generation runs on the gpt-image models, on every plan including Free.

OpenAI has also started putting ChatGPT inside Microsoft Office. Its help centre lists ChatGPT for Word, ChatGPT for Excel and ChatGPT for PowerPoint alongside Codex and ChatGPT Work as places credits can be spent, and third-party coverage describes them as a single Microsoft add-in available on every plan including Free. OpenAI's own pages do not say what these products are or which plans include them, which is why I am describing the coverage rather than asserting the feature.

One sourcing note, and it is a correction. The August version of this post said OpenAI returns HTTP 403 to automated access on its own site, so every OpenAI figure was second-hand. That is no longer the case. OpenAI's help centre answered every request I made on 24 November, which is where the Plus and Pro prices in this post come from. The pricing page answered too, it just does not print prices. The OpenAI half of this comparison is now better sourced than the Google half, which is the reverse of what I told you in August.

## Head to head, corrected again

**Writing.** ChatGPT, for prose that needs to sound like a person wrote it. Gemini reads more structured and more formal, which suits reports and hurts blog posts. If writing is the whole job, [Claude](/blog/claude-ai-review) beats both.

**Research and current information.** Gemini, on the strength of native search. Deep Research will spend several minutes across dozens of sources and hand back a cited report. Both still hallucinate, so verify anything that matters.

**Images.** Closer than it was, and I still have not tested the current engines against each other. Nano Banana Pro against the gpt-image models is a different matchup from Imagen 3 against DALL-E. I am not going to tell you which wins on quality when I have not run them.

**Coding.** ChatGPT edges it in the chat, and both are the wrong tool for real project work. Use Codex or Claude Code in a terminal. If you want the assistant inside your editor, [Cursor vs GitHub Copilot](/blog/cursor-vs-github-copilot) prices the two main options properly.

**Voice.** Both do real spoken conversation. This used to be a clear ChatGPT win and it is no longer a deciding factor.

**Your email and your calendar.** Gemini, decisively, and this is now the narrowest and most durable claim I can make about Workspace. ChatGPT has arrived inside Word, Excel and PowerPoint, so the old flat statement that it does not compete in productivity apps is wrong. It still does not read your inbox.

**Long documents.** Gemini has the reputation and probably still the edge, but I have stopped quoting a context number as the reason. Vendors' own pages disagree with each other about their windows. Test with your documents.

**Video.** Gemini takes video uploads up to 2 GB each, with five minutes of total attachment length on the free tier and an hour on AI Pro or Ultra, and generates video on paid tiers through Omni Flash. Those limits are from Google's own help page and they are tighter than the impression the August version of this post gave. On the OpenAI side, the help pages I read do not address video analysis either way, so it stays an open question rather than a flat no.

**Not seeing ads.** ChatGPT, oddly enough, if you pay. Gemini's free tier has no advertising that Google documents, and ChatGPT's does. But any paid ChatGPT tier removes ads entirely, and that is a cleaner promise than the Ads-Free option on Free, which costs you usage.

## The ecosystem gap, and how much it still matters

This is the argument ChatGPT loyalists reach for, and it is half as strong as it used to be.

ChatGPT's third-party store is genuinely larger. Thousands of purpose-built assistants, some excellent, most abandoned. Gems, Google's equivalent, is a smaller and quieter collection, and building your own takes more setup for less payoff.

But the reason people cited that store was to get behaviour the base product could not do, and the base products got much better. Custom instructions, memory, and project-scoped context now cover most of what a mid-tier custom assistant was for. I used to keep four or five specialised assistants and now use none, because telling the model what I want in a project instruction is faster than finding the right prebuilt one.

Where the gap is still real is the long tail. If your profession has a niche tool someone built and maintains, ChatGPT probably has it and Gemini probably does not. If your needs are ordinary, the store is a feature you will stop opening after a fortnight.

Deep Research runs the other way. Google's version will spend several minutes building a research plan, working through dozens of sources, and returning something structured and cited. It is the single Gemini feature I would miss most, and it is the closest either product comes to replacing an afternoon of work rather than a paragraph of typing. It is not perfect: it over-trusts sources that agree with each other, and it will cite an aggregator as readily as a primary source, which is exactly the failure mode I keep running into when checking facts for this site.

## Which one handles what

The practical version, based on what I actually reach for.

**Preparing from a briefing document.** Gemini for anything long. ChatGPT is fine under about forty pages.

**Drafting a blog post.** ChatGPT, then heavy editing. [Claude](/blog/write-blog-post-faster-with-ai) if prose quality is the whole point.

**Researching a company or a market.** Gemini's Deep Research, or [Perplexity](/blog/perplexity-ai-review) if you want every claim sourced as you go.

**Drafting an email to someone you have emailed before.** Gemini with Workspace, no contest. It has the thread and ChatGPT does not.

**Debugging a specific error.** Either, in a chat. Both run code, so the old ChatGPT advantage here has gone.

**Summarising a recording.** Gemini, with the caveat I did not have in August. Five minutes of video on the free tier and an hour on AI Pro is not a two-hour meeting. Check the length before you plan around it.

**Anything on your phone, hands-free, on Android.** Gemini, because it replaced Assistant and reaches your calendar and contacts.

**Generating an image plus copy in one pass.** ChatGPT, on ecosystem convenience rather than image quality, which I have not retested since both engines changed.

**Making a slide deck.** Genuinely neither, still, even though ChatGPT now appears inside PowerPoint. Use a dedicated tool.

## What I would tell someone starting fresh

If you already pay Google for storage, start with an AI plan there. The marginal cost is small and you get the Workspace integration nobody else offers.

If you write for a living and are not tied to Google's apps, ChatGPT Plus, and consider whether [Claude](/blog/claude-ai-review) is actually the better buy for that specific job.

If you are a student or on a tight budget, use the free tiers, and go in knowing what each one costs you now that neither is simply free. Google's free tier gives you Hydrogen rather than the model its paying customers get. ChatGPT's free tier may show you ads, and the way out of them on Free is a smaller allowance. In August I told readers Google's free offer was better than it looked. I would not say that today.

If you are a developer, neither of these chat windows is where the work happens. Look at the terminal and editor tools instead.

## The two claims I am retracting

**First, the free tier.** This post said Google's own help pages state that every tier, including free, gets the same three models, and that paying buys more usage rather than a better brain. I called it the most underrated thing about Gemini and built a section around it.

Google's plan pages now tie models to tiers, with Hydrogen on Free and Carbon reserved for Ultra. I do not know the exact date that changed, because I was not re-reading the page between August and November. I know it had changed by 16 November, because I read the cards that day for a different post and wrote down what they said. This post kept arguing the opposite for another eight days. That is the failure, and it is not Google's: a claim about a vendor's gating policy is exactly the kind of thing that needs re-reading before it gets repeated.

**Second, the context window.** The pre-August version led on a 1,000,000 token Gemini context window against roughly 128,000 on ChatGPT Plus, described as not a subtle difference. I retracted that in August and it stays retracted. The numbers move with the model you pick, and vendors' own documentation contradicts itself on them. Gemini is still likely the better long-document tool, which is a weaker claim than the one I made and the one I can support.

I have kept a running list of this kind of decay in [the stale model article audit](/blog/ai-model-articles-stale-audit), and this post is now in it twice.

## Who should pick which

**Google AI Pro** if your work lives in Gmail, Docs and Drive, if you want the cheaper entry point, if you are on Android, or if you already pay for Google storage.

**ChatGPT Plus** if you want the third-party ecosystem, if prose quality decides it for you, if you are already fluent in its habits, or if you would rather not be advertised to.

**ChatGPT Pro 500** only if you already know why. If you are reading a comparison article to decide, it is not for you.

**Start free on either, with your eyes open.** This is still better advice than it was six months ago, and it needs more caveats than it did in August. Google's free tier no longer matches its paid models. ChatGPT's free tier may show ads, and the ad-free version of it has lower limits. Spend a fortnight on both and notice where you actually hit a ceiling.

**Neither, if you mostly want cited research.** [Perplexity](/blog/perplexity-ai-review) does that better than either and is a different way of working rather than a better version of the same thing.

## My honest take

I use ChatGPT for writing and reach for Gemini when I need current information or something out of my own Google account. That has not changed in a year and I expect it will not change next year.

What changed is the story I would tell you about why. In August the tidy version was that the prices had converged and the difference was what each one gated: Google sold you volume, OpenAI sold you access. It was a satisfying symmetry and it lasted about three months. OpenAI went up to $500 and Google started gating models. The symmetry is gone and what replaced it is messier: two companies that both meter in credits at prices they will not publish, on pricing pages that no longer print prices, selling models that change name every few weeks.

The part I keep coming back to is not about either company. It is that I argued both sides of the free tier question on this site for eight days without noticing, because one post was getting re-read and the other was not. Every fact in the Gemini half of this post was correct when I wrote it and quietly stopped being correct, and nothing on the page warned anyone. That is the normal condition of writing about this industry, not an unusual lapse, which is the uncomfortable bit.

Check the date on anything you read about either product, this included. Then check the vendor's page before you pay, and be ready for it not to tell you the price.
